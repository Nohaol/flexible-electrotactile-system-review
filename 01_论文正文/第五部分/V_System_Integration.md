## V. System Integration

### V.1 From isolated stimulation to a wearable feedback chain

A flexible electrotactile interface becomes a system only when sensing, computation, stimulus generation, the skin interface and the user task are coupled in one operating loop. A minimal chain contains an external object or body state, a sensing module, signal acquisition and preprocessing, a tactile encoder, a multichannel stimulus driver, a flexible electrode-skin interface, human perception and a behavioral or machine-control update. Each block has its own performance metrics, but the useful question is how the output of one block constrains the next. A fast tactile rendering circuit, for example, does not define the latency of a closed-loop interaction unless sensing integration time, wireless transmission, channel scanning, perceptual decision and task response are also measured.

This system view also prevents a common category error: a device that combines a sensor and a stimulator is not automatically closed loop. The closed variable, the update rule and the task endpoint must be specified. In myoelectric prosthesis control, Dosen and colleagues used time-division multiplexing to separate electromyographic recording from electrotactile stimulation on the same limb, showing that stimulation artifacts, feedback-window length and control performance must be considered together rather than optimized as independent modules ([Dosen et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1186/1743-0003-11-138)). In high-density fingertip rendering, Lin and colleagues coupled an electrode array, current steering and output monitoring to improve spatial rendering, but their device-level refresh rate should still be distinguished from end-to-end task latency ([Lin et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abp8738)). Fig. 8 should therefore represent a chain of measured interfaces, not a collage of components.

### V.2 Skin-integrated platforms as the mechanical and electronic base

The first integration problem is not the pulse waveform but the wearable platform that carries conductors, electronics, power paths and contact interfaces on moving skin. Epidermal electronics established the design logic of ultrathin, low-stiffness systems that laminate to skin through conformal contact and integrate sensing, active electronics, radio-frequency components, wireless coils and solar cells in skin-mounted formats ([Kim et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1206157)). This platform evidence is not electrotactile feedback evidence by itself; its value for this review is to show why electrode placement, interconnect mechanics, power routing and communication cannot be treated as external accessories.

Plastic bioelectronics extends the same argument at the materials and manufacturing level. Flexible substrates, soft electronic materials, large-area processing, active-matrix addressing and mixed electronic-ionic conductors can support multipoint sensing or stimulation on curvilinear biological surfaces ([Someya et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature21004)). For electrotactile haptics, this means that the material stack is part of the stimulation path. A thinner or more compliant substrate may improve contact and comfort, while dense routing, encapsulation, sweat exposure and local rigid islands can alter impedance, reliability and the user's tolerance during motion. Sections II and IV describe the interface physics and materials; the role of Section V is to ask whether the assembled platform preserves the intended stimulus under wearable conditions.

### V.3 Sensing, encoding and multichannel driving

The sensing side of an electrotactile system determines what information can be fed back. Contact location, pressure, slip, joint motion or remote machine state each demand different sampling rates, dynamic ranges and preprocessing. The encoder must then map those signals into electrode position, pulse amplitude, pulse width, frequency, burst timing or discharge events. That mapping is limited by psychophysical thresholds, body-site variation, electrode geometry and prior stimulation history, rather than by the nominal number of physical electrodes alone.

The driver translates encoded commands into actual charge delivery. For array systems, the relevant variables include the number of physical electrodes, the number of simultaneously addressable channels, line count, switch topology, channel isolation, delivered phase charge, compliance voltage and output monitoring. A high-compliance stimulator can be necessary when noninvasive loads have high and variable impedance, but the rated voltage is not a recommended skin voltage or a safety threshold. Trout and colleagues reported a portable programmable multichannel stimulator designed with high compliance for human noninvasive neural stimulation; in this review it supports the driver-design requirement, not a universal electrotactile operating point ([Trout et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-30545-8)). Similarly, current steering and impedance-aware monitoring in high-resolution electrotactile rendering support the idea that spatial resolution, driver electronics and the electrode-skin interface are a single design problem ([Lin et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abp8738)).

Consequently, Table 4 should avoid ranking systems by electrode count, open-circuit voltage or nominal refresh rate alone. A useful comparison row should report electrode geometry and body site, physical and effective perceptual channels, driving mode, actual load waveform, phase charge or discharge energy, calibration procedure, impedance or contact monitoring, wireless or wired control, power source, task endpoint and user-study conditions. Values reported under different body sites, pulse definitions, loads and tasks can be placed side by side as evidence fields, but not merged into a single performance leaderboard.

### V.4 Power supply, harvesting and self-powered stimulation

Power integration cuts across all modules. A wearable electrotactile device may be wired, battery powered, wirelessly powered, energy harvesting, or self-powered for a specific function. These labels are not interchangeable. A wireless system can still depend on a battery; a battery-free receiver can still rely on an external transmitter; a self-powered sensor does not imply self-powered stimulation; and a triboelectric generator that can trigger a perceptible discharge under motion does not prove continuous operation at rest. Fig. 7 should therefore draw energy sources, storage, regulation, switching and the actual skin stimulus as separate elements.

Shi and colleagues demonstrated a self-powered electro-tactile system in which a triboelectric nanogenerator drove a ball-shaped electrode interface through controlled air-gap discharge; they reported that a 4 cm2 TENG contact area could trigger discharge and that electrode-skin distance regulated the induced current ([Shi et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abe2943)). This is a direct self-powered stimulation route, not merely self-powered sensing. Its system boundary is nevertheless specific: the stimulus depends on mechanical actuation, TENG output, gap geometry, discharge conditions and the tested skin interface. It should be used to define a category and constraints, not to claim general energy autonomy for every virtual tactile display.

The self-powered electrotactile textile haptic glove by Xu and colleagues pushes this route toward a wearable human-machine interface. The reported chain is hand motion -> textile TENG -> power management with a gas discharge tube -> skin electrode -> localized electrotactile sensation. In the extracted paper, the authors report impulse currents in the 0.2 to 4.8 mA range, a 120 V gas-discharge-tube test condition with a 10 nF capacitor, and a participant study that graded arm sensations from no feeling to painful across voltage and capacitance settings ([Xu et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adt0318)). The same evidence also shows why energy and perception must be reported together: the paper describes gender- and site-dependent sensation maps and a wide energy range from imperceptible to painful. Thus SPETH supports the claim that daily movement can power localized electrotactile feedback under tested conditions, but it does not remove the need for individual calibration, task-specific timing measurement or long-duration skin-safety validation.

### V.5 Closed-loop HMI evaluation and system-level trade-offs

The final test of integration is whether feedback improves a defined human-machine task without imposing unacceptable burden. Touch-IoT-type systems are valuable because they show bidirectional sensing, wireless transmission and haptic reproduction as an interaction architecture, even when a particular output module is mechanical rather than electrotactile ([Li et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.ade2450)). Conversely, the SPETH glove is directly electrotactile and self-powered at the stimulation route, and the paper demonstrates prosthetic-feedback and robotic-interaction scenarios in which contact events from a robotic hand can be delivered to the participant's arm ([Xu et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adt0318)). A closed-loop drone-control interface further illustrates that multimodal sensing and tactile feedback can be evaluated in a task loop, but its neuromuscular or force-feedback elements should not be relabelled as cutaneous electrotactile perception ([Yiu et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adt6041)).

Across these cases, the important trade-offs are cross-layer. More channels can increase spatial addressability while also increasing routing, switching, power and crosstalk burdens. Stronger or more reliable stimulation can require higher compliance, larger stored energy or stricter protection. Lower latency may demand faster sensing, computation, communication and channel scanning, each of which can raise power consumption or reduce robustness. Softer, more breathable or more textile-like formats can improve wearability while complicating encapsulation, interconnect density and contact stability. Greater energy autonomy can reduce external hardware but introduces intermittency, storage and load-matching constraints. A system claim is credible only when these trade-offs are reported under the same boundary: the same body site, operating state, power path, stimulus definition and task.

Section V therefore links the earlier parts of the review. Section II explains why neural and perceptual outcomes cannot be inferred from terminal settings alone; Sections III and IV define feasible encoding and material routes; Section V asks whether the assembled hardware, energy path and feedback task preserve those assumptions during use. The chapter should close with a practical evaluation rule: a flexible electrotactile haptic interface is mature not when it contains many advanced parts, but when it can deliver calibrated, comfortable, repeatable and task-relevant feedback through a wearable system boundary that is explicitly measured.
