## II.2 Electrode–Skin Interface and Equivalent Models

### II.2.1 The interface as a transfer path

Section II.1 related stimulation settings to perceptual endpoints, but those settings do not specify the electrical conditions at an excitable membrane. Between the output stage and underlying tissue lie the electrode, any electrolyte or air gap, the stratum corneum, viable skin and a return path. The stratum corneum is a substantial barrier at low frequencies; appendages and spatially nonuniform contact add parallel pathways, and tissue geometry determines how injected current spreads. Consequently, the voltage measured at a pair of surface electrodes contains contributions from several interfaces and tissue regions, rather than a unique “skin resistance” ([Rosell et al., 1988](https://doi.org/10.1109/10.4599); [Lu et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.3390/bios8020031)). A circuit fitted at the terminals can describe their electrical response without locating the excited afferent or predicting a sensation.

This distinction matters for the driving mode. For an ideal voltage source with a fixed electrode geometry, a change in the composite impedance can alter the current waveform; for an ideal current source, the terminal voltage must change to sustain the commanded current. Neither idealization guarantees an invariant extracellular field: current can redistribute among local pathways, and a real current driver can reach its voltage-compliance limit. In a transcutaneous stimulation experiment using hydrogel electrodes on the thigh, Vargas Luna and colleagues recorded different within-pulse current–voltage responses for voltage-controlled and current-controlled biphasic pulses and fitted an intensity- and charge-dependent interface model ([Vargas Luna et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0125609)). Their fit validates electrical waveforms under those conditions, not a mapping from terminal impedance to recruited fibers or perceived intensity.

### II.2.2 What the equivalent circuits represent

A useful first approximation places a deeper-tissue resistance in series with a parallel stratum-corneum resistance and capacitance: $Z_{\mathrm{SC}}(\omega)=\left(R_{\mathrm{SC}}^{-1}+j\omega C_{\mathrm{SC}}\right)^{-1}$. The resistive branch represents conductive leakage through the barrier, while the capacitive branch represents charge displacement; neither component is a separately imaged anatomical channel. The contact and electrode must then be represented according to the actual material and wetting state: an ohmic contact resistance may be useful when an ionic or conductive junction dominates, but it cannot simply replace a capacitive dry gap. In dry metal recording electrodes, Goyal and colleagues modelled the oxide and microscopic air-gap contact as a **series contact capacitance**, followed by $R_{\mathrm{SC}}\parallel C_{\mathrm{SC}}$ and deeper-tissue resistance ([Goyal et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/s22218510)). In pre-gelled Ag/AgCl recording electrodes, Murphy and colleagues instead separated a charge-transfer resistance in parallel with double-layer capacitance at the electrode, gel resistance, an epidermal $R\parallel C$ branch and subcutaneous resistance ([Murphy et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/s21155210)). These are condition-specific terminal models, not interchangeable drawings of a universal interface (Fig. 3b).

Electrode polarization describes voltage associated with charge storage and, where applicable, charge-transfer reactions at the electrode–electrolyte boundary. A double-layer capacitance can approximate the storage branch, whereas a finite charge-transfer resistance represents a faradaic path in an appropriate electrochemical model; a dry capacitive contact need not contain the same gel/electrolyte branch. A constant-phase element (CPE), $Z_{\mathrm{CPE}}=1/[Q(j\omega)^{\alpha}]$ with $0<\alpha\leq1$, can fit a distributed, nonideal frequency response; $\alpha=1$ gives an ideal capacitance with $Q=C$. In Goyal's dry-electrode study, CPEs were used in fitting the stratum-corneum and contact branches even though the physical explanation was presented in terms of effective capacitances ([Goyal et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/s22218510)). A CPE parameter or a fitted RC time constant should therefore be interpreted within its circuit and frequency window, rather than labelled an intrinsic property of a particular skin layer. More elaborate layered and appendage-aware circuits can represent additional pathways, at the cost of more parameters that may not be uniquely identifiable from one electrode arrangement ([Lu et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.3390/bios8020031)).

### II.2.3 Frequency-domain measurements and stimulation pulses

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measures the terminal response to a small alternating perturbation over specified frequencies and can help distinguish dispersive branches when the assumed circuit fits the data. Rosell and colleagues measured gelled, unabraded skin at ten body sites in ten people from 1 Hz to 1 MHz: the approximately 1-cm² measurement showed roughly 10 kΩ–1 MΩ at 1 Hz, whereas the high-frequency values converged near 120 Ω at 1 MHz ([Rosell et al., 1988](https://doi.org/10.1109/10.4599)). These values establish strong frequency and site dependence for that measurement geometry; they are neither wearable-device design constants nor electrotactile thresholds. Likewise, Murphy's 10-mV-rms, 1-Hz–100-kHz EIS tracked pre-gelled electrodes on the upper arm at placement, 8 h and 24 h ([Murphy et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/s21155210)). Frequency-domain fits are informative about that small-signal state but need not remain valid throughout a much larger stimulation pulse (Fig. 3c).

Pulse experiments address a different question: how the terminal current and voltage evolve as charge is delivered. Vargas Luna and colleagues applied 30-ms-per-phase, charge-balanced biphasic pulses through 25- and 50-cm² hydrogel electrodes, under both current and voltage control. Their simplified circuit comprised a series resistance and a parallel RC branch whose effective conductance changed with stimulation current and cumulative charge; it reproduced recorded electrical responses with reported coefficients of determination around 0.99 ([Vargas Luna et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0125609)). That result does not license transferring its fitted parameters to the much shorter pulses, dry electrodes or anatomical sites used in every flexible electrotactile display. It also does not establish that electroporation or electro-osmosis was directly observed in individual skin pathways. The modelling mechanisms are plausible explanations for terminal dynamics, with their contributions still dependent on protocol and model assumptions.

### II.2.4 Contact, hydration and time as operating variables

Effective area, pressure, roughness, material and moisture change the contact geometry and the barrier state, while motion can vary them during wear. In Goyal's controlled dry-phantom comparison, increasing stainless-steel electrode area from 4 to 9 cm² reduced fitted $R_{\mathrm{SC}}$ from 1.62 to 0.70 MΩ and increased fitted contact capacitance from 0.68 to 1.34 µF. In their dry-versus-hydrated phantom comparison, area-normalized fitted $R_{\mathrm{SC}}$ decreased from 6.4 to 3.0 MΩ cm² and effective $C_{\mathrm{SC}}$ increased from 41 to 190 nF cm⁻² ([Goyal et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/s22218510)). These are fitted recording-interface parameters under specified phantom conditions, not direct stimulation dose or comfort measurements. They nevertheless show why electrode design and contact state cannot be omitted when comparing nominal pulse settings.

Time adds another scale of variation. In 14 participants wearing pre-gelled upper-arm recording electrodes, Murphy and colleagues found that the initial impedance differed by skin treatment but, after 24 h, the groups had converged to less than 20 kΩ at 10 Hz; their fitted epidermal and electrode parameters changed over time ([Murphy et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/s21155210)). Hydration under the electrode is a plausible contributor in that study, whereas an individual sweat event, pressure shift or movement artifact was not isolated as a cause of electrotactile threshold change. Thus a lower or more stable terminal impedance can support more reproducible electrical delivery, but it does not by itself prove a lower sensation threshold, selective neural recruitment, comfort or safety (Fig. 3d).

### II.2.5 Consequences for stimulation and feedback

Interface-aware design starts by specifying which quantity is regulated and where it is measured: commanded current, actual electrode current, terminal voltage, delivered phase charge and, if available, a diagnostic impedance estimate are different variables. Current regulation can make phase charge more repeatable when the driver remains within compliance, but a rising interface voltage increases compliance and output-stage demands; voltage regulation avoids that particular current-source constraint while permitting delivered current to vary with the interface. Biphasic charge balancing, output monitoring, fault handling and re-calibration must be evaluated for the actual electrode and waveform rather than inferred from a single EIS number. Kajimoto reported a real-time impedance-feedback electrotactile display that adjusted pulse width and reduced spatial and temporal variability in an evaluation; the available abstract establishes a feedback demonstration, not universal perceptual stabilization or safety ([Kajimoto, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1109/TOH.2011.39)). The central system implication is that electrical sensing, pulse delivery and psychophysical calibration need to be co-designed: terminal measurements constrain what was delivered, while user-specific perceptual tests remain necessary to establish what was felt. Sections III and IV address stimulus control and flexible interface construction, respectively; Section V returns to these coupled requirements at the multichannel wearable-system level.
