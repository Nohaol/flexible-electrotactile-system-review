## II.1 Physiological Mechanism of Electrotactile Perception

In natural touch, a mechanical event deforms the skin and is transduced by cutaneous low-threshold mechanoreceptive afferents. Their activity reaches spinal and supraspinal circuits, where signals from afferents with different response properties are integrated into tactile perception. This sequence provides a physiological reference for electrotactile interfaces: what reaches the nervous system matters more than the nominal stimulus applied at the skin surface. Natural touch begins with mechanically sensitive peripheral structures, whereas an electrotactile interface introduces an electrical input before afferent signaling ([Abraira and Ginty, 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2013.07.051); [Kaczmarek et al., 1991](https://doi.org/10.1109/10.68204)).

For transcutaneous electrotactile stimulation, the stimulator drives current through an electrode and the skin, establishing an extracellular potential distribution in the underlying tissue. A sufficiently effective field can polarize excitable neural membranes and initiate or modulate afferent activity without first requiring the mechanical deformation and mechanotransduction of natural touch. The relation between extracellular field, membrane polarization and excitation is supported by neural stimulation models, but those models do not identify the first activated structure beneath a particular wearable electrode. Moreover, measurements made during transcutaneous biphasic pulses show that the electrode–skin interface can change the delivered current–voltage waveform within and across pulses ([Rattay, 1999](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0306-4522(98)00330-3); [Vargas Luna et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0125609)). Thus, the programmed output is neither a direct measurement of membrane polarization nor a complete description of the neural input.

Neural recruitment should consequently be separated from the sensation that a participant reports. Myelinated Aβ afferents are central to many forms of discriminative touch, while low-threshold mechanoreceptive Aδ and C afferents also occur in hairy skin; these natural categories do not establish which fibers a surface electrode selects. Models predict that the site and extent of electrical excitation depend on the field and neural geometry, whereas electrotactile studies commonly measure detection, intensity or perceived quality rather than recording identified afferents ([Abraira and Ginty, 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2013.07.051); [Rattay, 1999](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0306-4522(98)00330-3); [Marcus and Fuglevand, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1088/1741-2560/6/6/066008)). Descriptors such as tingling, pressure or pain therefore cannot, by themselves, establish selective Aβ, Aδ or C-fiber activation. For the surface-electrode conditions considered here, the initial activation site and subtype-specific recruitment remain insufficiently resolved by direct neural evidence.

At a fixed electrode configuration and waveform, increasing pulse amplitude is expected to increase the applied field, while increasing pulse width prolongs each phase and, for an ideal rectangular current pulse, increases delivered phase charge. Both changes can alter the probability of a detectable response, but their perceptual effects are conditional on the actual waveform and tissue coupling. In a study of biphasic current pulses on the upper arm, the peak-current thresholds for perception, attention and intolerance all decreased as pulse width increased; this result does not imply that longer pulses improve comfort or reduce total delivered charge ([Dölker et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-10708-9)). On the dorsal neck, perceived-intensity estimates were more sensitive to current-amplitude modulation than to pulse-frequency modulation under the tested pulse-train conditions ([Marcus and Fuglevand, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1088/1741-2560/6/6/066008)). These are measured psychophysical relationships, not direct measurements of how many or which afferents were recruited.

Pulse frequency changes the temporal sequence of electrical inputs and, for a fixed train duration, the number of pulses delivered. In Van Doren's equal-perceived-amplitude contours, the current adjustment needed to hold perceived amplitude constant changed relatively little with pulse rate across the tested 4–64 Hz range; this is a statement about a matched percept, not a frequency sweep at fixed current ([Van Doren, 1997](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03211869)). A separate fingertip dissimilarity experiment resolved perceived frequency and intensity as related but distinct dimensions ([Kaczmarek et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1109/TNSRE.2017.2702628)). Together, these results make pulse rate a distinct psychophysical variable whose effect depends on the waveform, amplitude and judgment task. Repeated stimulation adds a history-dependent effect: electrotactile conditioning on the abdomen raised subsequent sensation thresholds and reduced perceived magnitude under the tested conditions ([Kaczmarek, 2000](https://doi.org/10.1109/86.895953)). These perceptual findings do not identify the underlying neural firing pattern.

The strength–duration relationship formalizes one aspect of this parameter dependence by relating the stimulus amplitude required to reach a specified endpoint to pulse duration. A Weiss–Lapicque-type approximation, $I_{\mathrm{th}}(t) \approx I_{\mathrm{r}}\left(1+t_{\mathrm{c}}/t\right)$, describes threshold current $I_{\mathrm{th}}$ for pulse duration $t$, with rheobase $I_{\mathrm{r}}$ and fitted chronaxie $t_{\mathrm{c}}$. In a forearm sensation experiment, rectangular and capacitor-discharge cathodal pulses produced different threshold curves, and the mean fitted chronaxie for the capacitor-discharge waveform was approximately twice that of the rectangular waveform ([Wessale et al., 1992](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02368523)). This perceptual comparison shows why a fitted chronaxie is conditional on waveform and endpoint; it is not a measurement of the intrinsic membrane time constant of an identified fiber. Electrode geometry, body site, skin condition and driving mode also need to accompany a reported strength–duration parameter.

Threshold terminology is equally consequential. Detection, sensation or perception threshold denotes the criterion used to establish that a participant notices a stimulus; an attention threshold denotes a different behavioral judgment, and discomfort, pain and intolerance need not occur at the same amplitude. In 81 volunteers receiving upper-arm stimulation, Dölker and colleagues reported median perception, attention and intolerance thresholds of 3.5, 6.9 and 13.8 mA, respectively, for their 150-µs biphasic stimulus ([Dölker et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-10708-9)). Their study also notes that pain could be reported before intolerance and that visible muscle twitch sometimes terminated threshold testing. By comparison, Marcus and Fuglevand called the onset of reported discomfort during neck stimulation a “pain threshold,” illustrating why similarly named upper limits cannot automatically be pooled ([Marcus and Fuglevand, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1088/1741-2560/6/6/066008)). None of these group values defines a universal comfortable operating range or an electrical safety limit; those require separately specified perceptual and tissue or device criteria.

The resulting thresholds differ across people and stimulation locations, but the sources of variation must be identified rather than compressed into a single “skin sensitivity” factor. Five positions on the forearm yielded different perception thresholds in one multi-electrode experiment, whereas upper-arm electrode position in another study affected the tested intolerance endpoint more clearly than perception or attention thresholds ([Geng et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1186/1743-0003-8-9); [Dölker et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-10708-9)). Local innervation, hydration, electrode contact and individual reporting may all contribute, while repeated exposure can shift the perceptual baseline ([Abraira and Ginty, 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2013.07.051); [Vargas Luna et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0125609); [Dölker et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-10708-9); [Kaczmarek, 2000](https://doi.org/10.1109/86.895953)). These studies do not isolate the contribution of each factor. Even with identical stimulator settings, the effective electrical input delivered through the skin to excitable tissue need not be identical; this transfer problem motivates Section II.2, Electrode–Skin Interface and Equivalent Models ([Vargas Luna et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0125609)).
